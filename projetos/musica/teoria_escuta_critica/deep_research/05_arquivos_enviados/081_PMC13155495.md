# Practical Approaches to Balancing Music and Safety in the Operating Room: A Systematic and Narrative Review

Fonte: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13155495/ (texto integral via Europe PMC / NCBI)

## Abstract

AbstractBackground:Music in the operating room (OR) reduces stress and improves focus and team performance. It can also distract the team, increase miscommunication, and threaten patient safety. Previous work has characterized these pros and cons eloquently, yet no cohesive guideline to manage OR music responsibly exists to date. Here, a practical framework for OR music that reduces risks and maximizes benefits is proposed.Methods:Both scoping and systematic quantitative review guidelines, including Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses, were queried using search terms to identify the effects of OR music on surgical teams and whether existing regulatory guidelines were available.Results:Fourteen studies met the inclusion criteria for the systematic review, including randomized/crossover trials (29%), controlled nonrandomized studies (14%), observational studies (43%), and cross-sectional surveys (14%). Across these, surgeon-directed music was associated with improved mood, reduced stress, and greater perceived efficiency, whereas team-level outcomes showed mixed effects, with 9 of 14 studies (64%) reporting impaired communication, distraction, or reduced situational awareness. No study provided a formal guideline for OR music management beyond general suggestions, such as low volume and instrumental selections.Conclusions:Managing OR music is necessary to maintain patient safety, and doing so using grassroots principles, starting with team involvement, could provide a better and more sustainable solution.

## INTRODUCTION

Music in the operating room (OR) has been shown to calm the mind and improve performance, especially in other high-pressure environments.1–7 It can also potentially cause harm. Loud music interferes with the monitor alarms and impedes clear communication; lyric-heavy songs with offensive lyrics distract the team; and repeated music requests from the surgical team deters circulating nurses from doing their work.8–11 A misplaced focus on the playlist instead of the patient poses a threat to both patients and OR staff.12 A growing concern is also that surgical teams play OR music merely to fit in, and it has no positive influence at all.13,14 Despite these concerns, there is no coherent consensus on how to manage OR music in a way that harmonizes the team without hurting the patient. This review synthesizes current evidence on the benefits and risks of OR music and uses these findings to propose a practical, team-centered framework for safer and more effective implementation.

## METHODS

### Design

This work has 2 components: (1) a quantitative analysis and (2) a qualitative review of the literature, which are addressed in the following sections. Specifically, a systematic review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines was performed using search terms in PubMed/MEDLINE as the sole database. In addition, a scoping review of the literature was performed to assess qualitative data published in the English-language, peer-reviewed studies; gray literature was not included.

### Systematic Review for Quantitative Analysis

PubMed/MEDLINE was queried from database inception through 2025 using the following search string: (music[Mesh] OR music therapy[Mesh] OR music[tiab] OR noise[tiab] OR auditory environment[tiab] OR background sound[tiab] OR soundscape[tiab]) AND (operating rooms[Mesh] OR operating room[tiab] OR operating rooms[tiab] OR surgical suite[tiab] OR perioperative[tiab] OR intraoperative[tiab]) AND (patient safety[Mesh] OR safety management[Mesh] OR medical errors[Mesh] OR communication[tiab] OR teamwork[tiab] OR errors[tiab] OR adverse events[tiab]). Inclusion criteria included strictly case reports, clinical trials, comparative studies, controlled clinical trials, observational studies, practice guidelines, and randomized controlled trials. Of the total unique 196 records, all were screened at the title and abstract level. Reviews and meta-analyses were excluded, (n = 166). The remaining 30 full-text articles were tested for eligibility. Sixteen of them were excluded as they were not related to our topic, and the remaining 14 studies met all the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (Fig. 1).15 Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to decrease bias, then full texts, to identify eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. A standardized form was used to extract study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size), intervention details (type of music, timing, volume), and outcomes (patient physiological measures, adverse events, communication metrics, staff perceptions). Exploratory data analysis was conducted to best report findings. No formal methods were used to assess risk of bias due to missing results, as the included studies did not provide sufficient trial registration data or predefined protocols to permit such evaluation.

Fig. 1.Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses diagram of included articles for the systematic review.

### Scoping Review for Qualitative Analysis

The narrative/scoping review was conducted separately to capture broader perspectives on OR music. PubMed was searched for all relevant English-language articles, and gray literature and non-English studies were excluded. The search was conducted using controlled vocabulary and keywords related to “music,” “operating room,” “surgery,” “noise,” “patient safety,” and “communication.” Inclusion criteria were randomized controlled trials, nonrandomized studies, observational studies, and quality-improvement reports that (1) were conducted in an OR perioperative setting; (2) evaluated music or other auditory interventions on providers, not patients; (3) reported at least 1 patient safety, team communication, or physiological outcome (eg, adverse events, errors, surgical time, patient anxiety, hemodynamics); and (4) were published in English. We excluded studies conducted in postoperative wards, educational simulations, or those without outcome data. Our findings were qualitatively discussed and grouped by outcomes. Both types of reviews were reported following their own requirements: the systematic review as a data analysis, and the qualitative review in a narrative format.

## RESULTS

### Systematic Review Data

Figure 2 summarizes the characteristics of the 14 included articles. Of these, 6 (43%) were observational studies, 4 (29%) were randomized/crossover trials, 2 (14%) were controlled nonrandomized studies, and 2 (14%) were cross-sectional surveys. Subtopics that were most frequently studied included the effects of music or noise on communication and workload (n = 6; 43%), noise levels during cesarean deliveries (n = 2; 14%), music therapy for anxiety or sedation (n = 2; 14%), noise impact on clinical reasoning, and attitudes toward OR music and pediatric noise-reduction interventions (n = 2; 14%).

Fig. 2.Quantitative data from the systematic review.

Two types of topics were covered: (1) music used as a therapy for patient care in randomized or controlled designs, which reported reduced anxiety, lower sedative or analgesic requirements, and improved satisfaction, and (2) OR music used for improved ambiance in the OR in observational or simulation contexts. They reported impaired communication, increased workload, and cognitive distraction among surgical teams.

Of the 14 articles, 9 reported opinions that were not in favor of OR music and highlighted potential harms. Only 3 concluded that OR music should be encouraged and reported stress reduction and positive attitudes; 2 articles were “mixed,” noting mood benefits but also communication concerns (Fig. 3).

### Qualitative Review Data

Across the qualitative literature, OR music was described as having both beneficial and adverse effects on surgical teams. Several reports noted improved surgeon calmness, concentration, and workflow efficiency, particularly during routine procedures, with music contributing to reduced stress and a perceived state of flow.3,16 Figure 4 depicts a pros and cons list of the opinions in our findings.

Fig. 4.Pros and cons of playing music in the OR.

Conversely, multiple studies raised concerns regarding impaired communication, distraction during critical moments, and masking of important auditory cues.6,17,18 These concerns were more commonly reported by anesthesia professionals and nursing staff, who also described limited control over music selection and volume.19

When specified, lower volume instrumental or ambient music was perceived as less disruptive to communication and workflow.20,21 Although concerns were frequently reported, no study directly attributed adverse patient outcomes to the use of music in the OR. Finally, recommendations for the regulation of OR music were made by the Association of Perioperative Registered Nurses, but there is no specific published guideline or policy that has been reported.22,23

## DISCUSSION

Music in ORs, when used appropriately, offers benefits for surgeon performance, patient comfort, and team morale, but could distract the OR team and impair patient care. A balanced approach that maximizes the benefits of OR music while minimizing its risks is warranted, and our findings clearly indicate that this need is unmet.

These findings are directly relevant to plastic surgery, where providers are collaborating with a multitude of other specialists in a single case. As one of the most interdisciplinary and diverse specialties, team harmony regarding music in the OR is especially important to ensure surgical precision, healthy communication, and workflow efficiency.24

The literature search did not report direct patient harm from OR music, yet real-world events reported in the news outlets highlight how distraction can become dangerous. In 1 highly publicized medicolegal case, a routine cataract surgery ended in cardiac arrest after the surgical team was reportedly playing a “music bingo” game during the procedure. Audible alarms were allegedly silenced, abnormal vital signs went unnoticed for more than 10 minutes, and the patient later died.25 This case illustrates that while music can be beneficial, its misuse, particularly when it interferes with vigilance, alarm audibility, or team communication, can have catastrophic consequences. These events reinforce the need for careful, structured, and safety-conscious approaches to OR music.

An ideal form of regulation of OR music would be organically created by the very group that wants it, but also wants to avoid harm.26,27 Regulating OR music does not mean eliminating it completely but simply setting a mutually acceptable code of behavior. This approach builds consensus within the team and, thereby, is expected to elicit greater compliance than a rigid policy created and enforced by hospital administrators. A top-down policy to regulate music in the OR would be seen by surgeons and the OR team as an overreach and simply unfair.28 This sentiment would undoubtedly reduce the buy-in from the team.

Several real-world observations further demonstrate how the proposed framework can be applied in practice. Weldon et al11 reported that music-related communication delays led to repeated requests and added 4–68 seconds per interruption, illustrating how poor audibility and task distraction directly affect workflow. Similarly, higher intraoperative noise levels, including OR music, were associated with increased rates of surgical-site infections, highlighting noise as a modifiable patient safety factor.29 These concrete examples show that identifying team concerns, setting shared goals, and piloting small, team-driven adjustments are not theoretical ideals but practical steps that address measurable OR problems.

Informed by diverse literature, we present a simple strategy that is fundamentally rooted in provider autonomy, making the surgical team the primary drivers of this change. Specifically, this framework would comprise 4 steps (Fig. 5):

### Step 1: Identification of Team Concerns and Workflow Disruptions

Create a team to address the benefits and problems of OR music and how it affects each member of the OR team. This step is critical because it acknowledges that OR music has different effects depending on its role. For example, the circulating nurse is often asked to change the music or search a certain playlist—even though playing an OR-DJ is not their role. Further, it distracts them from doing their primary duties. The circulating nurse’s computer is the primary speaker in most ORs, and asking them to increase the volume creates a noisier environment for them than the rest of the team.

To achieve this goal, seeking input from the entire surgical team, including scrub technicians, circulating nurses, first assistants, certified registered nurse anesthetists, surgeons, and anesthesiologists, is imperative. It can be achieved through informal discussions, which work well in a smaller OR setting or an ambulatory surgery center. In a larger setting, such as a multispecialty hospital or academic medical center with several locations, a more formalized approach may be more effective. This includes web-based surveys or several smaller focus group meetings set up to identify the team’s needs and concerns.

It is also important to present available scientific evidence in an unbiased manner that includes the benefits of music on the team (calmness, concentration, stress reduction, and positive energy and team morale) as well as the negatives (distraction, noise pollution, and general disturbance). Demonstrating that music can have positive effects on performance and reduce stress may help staff view it as a tool for their benefit, rather than as a limitation. Similarly, open and honest acknowledgement of the risks helps the team understand that they are being heard and taken seriously.

To make these discussions focused and standardized, we have generated a brief questionnaire that asks some leading and other open-ended questions to seek team opinion (Fig. 6). Anonymity can also be ensured if there is a fear of targeting members who may be critical.

Fig. 6.Questionnaire to address opinions on OR music among team members.

### Step 2: Goal Setting

In this phase, the input from the OR team members is presented as data that can then drive decisions. First, emphasize that proposed modifications are for the patient’s benefit and consider how the OR team can serve them the best. Also, encourage the staff to ask patients for their preference, which highlights the patient-centeredness of this strategy.

Second, it must be acknowledged that music is an optional part of the OR environment, and the OR personnel can decide with implicit flexibility when music is deemed appropriate and when it is not. This empowers the staff and decreases any inherent resistance. Finally, the individual’s own apprehensions must be acknowledged and addressed.

### Step 3: Pilot Plan

Based on the input, a pilot music code can be implemented for a finite period to assess its impact and gather feedback from the surgical team. This will allow for adjustments based on real-world experiences. Periodic review and adjustments strengthen the team.Foster customization by encouraging individual teams to build consensus on the following:-Type of music that appeals to all and can be included in a playlist that works for everyone and does not distract.-Including different music tastes of the team members and asking each of them to select music of their choice can create a richer playlist. It will also make them feel heard and integrated.-Optimal volume, especially during the more critical parts of a case, ensures that team members themselves can balance the need for focus and concentration with the benefits of a calm environment. For example, some team members find noise distracting and may need a quieter environment to help them work efficiently. As such, noise levels in the OR are high, and using these guidelines as a measure could help select the right volume. For example, team members with hearing aids or sound sensitivity are unable to handle sound volume that others may not find troublesome.-Stopping the music during critical parts of the case is essential. These include the checklist, counts, or when a serious part of the operative course is reached.-Music that does not offend due to lyrics or styles. For example, many songs can have offensive lyrics, including curse words, racial slurs, and violent acts, any of which may not be acceptable to some team members. It must be emphasized that although it is not a one-size-fits-all recommendation, this framework can be tailored to suit each setting.-Leveraging new gadgets and artificial intelligence tools that could be voice-controlled, allowing the OR team the ability to give verbal commands for music volume, type, and even to switch off on command. Artificial intelligence tools could also be programmed to perform these tasks automatically when certain cues are manifest.

### Step 4: Periodic Assessment

Periodic assessment allows teams to evaluate whether the agreed-upon music practices are effective and safe. Brief check-ins or feedback cycles ensure that any concerns, communication issues, or workflow disruptions are identified early, and adjustments can be made promptly. This ongoing review keeps the system flexible, team-centered, and sustainable over time.

### Study Limitations

Existing OR music literature itself is limited by small sample sizes, observational study designs, and variable reporting quality. Across the included articles, standardized metrics for communication, distraction, and workflow were lacking, making cross-study comparisons difficult. In addition, the absence of preregistered protocols and inconsistent reporting of negative or null findings raise the possibility of publication bias in the available evidence.

Some might also argue that these outlines are too generic and oversimplified, and hence unlikely to work, as some might suggest a formal policy to regulate OR music and enforce strict compliance. Although a policy would certainly standardize OR music, whether it will change behavior is not guaranteed. In fact, it could backfire, with the team feeling discouraged and being told what they can and cannot do, without their input or their choice. A top-down policy may be perceived as reducing provider autonomy, which can limit engagement and adherence. Generally, employees resist change if they distrust their administrators or perceive an imbalance of power.30,31 Most effective changes are ones that are developed directly by the teams implementing them.32,33

Prior literature suggests that autonomy enhances engagement and satisfaction among clinical staff; therefore, overly rigid rules governing OR music may unintentionally reduce its perceived benefits.34 Finally, we believe that a rigid policy takes away the informal, playful nature of this activity and the choice of playing something the team enjoys and wants to share.

We agree that small focus groups could be ineffective if not held accountable; similarly, having a centralized work group decide what music one must listen to in the OR sounds just as contrived. Small focus groups with accountability could be more effective.35

The efficacy of the approach was not tested because its success is centrally linked to each OR team, which alone can set its own parameters with patient safety in mind. Because each site is unique, an overarching study would not reveal relevant guidelines to any specific site.

### Future Directions

Adding an informal postoperative debriefing of how the OR music affected the case could be a starting point for change. Adaptation of a wider variety of music selection with geographical and cultural differences could be a unique team-building exercise. Stratifying opinions and norms based on the culture of the practice location could help expand our individual tastes and enhance team cohesion.

## CONCLUSIONS

OR music either enhances or hinders team performance and could affect patient safety. Self-regulatory methods that engage the team could potentially elicit meaningful change.
